Research

What does it mean to know a robot? In Isaac Asimov’s I, Robot, Dr. Susan Calvin’s words echo the questions that guide my research. Is a robot simply “gears and metal,” or do we attribute a mind to it? When we see robots as “more faithful, more useful,” how does that shape our willingness to trust and rely on them? And if they are “human-made,” does that give us the right to “destroy” them?

By creating robots as intelligent as themselves, humans would be “no longer alone” in the universe. This could open new possibilities for cooperation and mutual flourishing, but also give rise to conflict and tension. How can we shape a future in which humans and robots coexist and thrive together?

I study how people understand AI and robots, trust and cooperate with them, and judge their morality and decisions. My research examines how mind perception and mental models shape these relationships, how trust develops and recovers after errors, and how moral expectations differ for artificial agents and humans. I also investigate the moral standing people grant artificial agents and how this shapes our responsibilities toward them. I approach these questions through psychology, communication, and cognitive science.
